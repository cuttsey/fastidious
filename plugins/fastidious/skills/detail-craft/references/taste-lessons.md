# Taste lessons: generalised

Principles extracted from studied work, written so they apply to any subject. Each lesson names the principle, why it works, how to apply it, and how it fails. Sources are listed at the end of each lesson.

## Colour

### 1. A palette is one temperature, played as a system
Choose three or four hues that sit close together (analogous) and one dark that is *tinted toward them* rather than neutral black (a chocolate brown for a warm palette, an ink-blue for a cool one). Every surface is a different mix of the same set: field, panel, accent, text.
- **Why:** closeness reads as harmony and a single mood; a tinted dark keeps text inside the family instead of punching holes in it.
- **Apply:** assign roles (ground, field, accent, ink) and rotate which hue plays which role from screen to screen. Variety comes from recombining, not adding.
- **Fails when:** a stray cool hue appears in a warm system; pure black text sits on warm fields; light text goes on mid-tone fields (check every pairing for contrast, especially white on orange and pale on yellow).
- *Source: a skincare brand-identity deck (butter yellow, tangerine, blush, chocolate brown).*

### 2. Grade the world to the palette
Photography, product backgrounds, props and environments are chosen or graded so their colours come from the palette (a blush tile wall, a lilac backdrop, warm skin tones).
- **Why:** images then belong to the system instead of interrupting it.
- **Fails when:** stock photos keep their own colour casts.

## Type

### 3. Give each face one job, and let one face carry the personality
A characterful display face (chunky, soft, retro, condensed, whatever the personality is) does the shouting; a neutral sans does the reading. A third voice, if used at all, appears **once per composition**: a single italic or script word that marks the turn ("Start *With* Good Skin").
- **Why:** contrast of roles creates hierarchy and personality at once; the single accent word works because it's rare.
- **Fails when:** the accent face spreads to whole lines, or two characterful faces compete.

### 4. Split headlines by colour or weight to build rhythm
Two-line headlines with a change of colour or weight per line ("Glow Your / Own Way", "Built Around / Better Skin Days") read as a beat and a payoff.
- **Fails when:** every headline does it (it stops meaning anything) or the split falls mid-phrase.

### 5. The wordmark is a graphic, not a signature
Use the name as type and as object: on packaging, as signage in a real space, at display size in layouts.
- **Why:** repetition in different materials proves the identity is real and memorable.

## Devices

### 6. Three repeated devices beat ten clever ones
Pick at most three graphic devices that come from the personality and use them everywhere, for example an offset colour plate behind rounded images, an organic blob, and a circular seal with text around it. Add small punctuation (a sparkle, a pill) sparingly.
- **Why:** a small set of repeated devices becomes recognisable; many one-offs read as template clip art.
- **Fails when:** devices crowd type, overlap text, or appear because a space looked empty.

### 7. Let images break their frames
Products, hands and figures overlap panel edges or burst out of their rounded frames; type overlaps images where contrast allows.
- **Why:** breaking the frame creates depth and energy without 3D.
- **Fails when:** overlaps obscure meaning or reduce contrast.

## Proof and content

### 8. Make proof huge and qualified
Big numerals with the qualifier directly beneath ("92% · noticed smoother skin after 4 weeks"), and benefit labels as small pills placed near the thing they describe.
- **Why:** specific numbers are believed; qualifiers make them honest. Pills turn claims into scannable tags.
- **Fails when:** a number has no qualifier or source.

### 9. Show the identity working in the world
Include applications: packaging, a shop interior, a person using the product, social posts. A system is proven by its use.

### 10. Direct the photography to one feeling
Every picture expresses the brand's core feeling (joy, calm, rigour): expression, posture, light and props agree. Real, varied people and product-in-hand beat staged perfection; one surreal prop (bottles on a driftwood "hand") makes it memorable.

## Composition and rhythm

### 11. Vary the layout, never the system
Each screen or slide uses a different composition (image left, image right, full bleed, colour-blocked panel, agenda list), but the palette, type roles and devices never change. Repetition of the system, variation of the layout.

### 12. Copy in threes, headlines as imperatives
Triplets ("Simple care · Real people · Good energy") and short imperatives ("Glow your own way") give voice a cadence. Keep body copy plain.

## Restraint and argument (from a brand studio's own site)

### 13. When the work is the colour, the frame is quiet
A studio or portfolio site that shows other people's identities uses one typeface (a neutral grotesk), black, white and **one** accent, so the work supplies all the colour and the site never competes with it.
- **Why:** restraint reads as confidence and lets every case look its best; a loud frame makes the work look smaller.
- **Fails when:** the frame has its own palette and devices fighting each tile, or the "restraint" is just empty (no idea, no accent, no voice).
- *Source: Primary (primary.studio): Suisse Int'l only, black and white, an electric lime accent.*

### 14. Give the accent one meaning, and a mark that carries it
The single accent stands for the core promise (lime = speed), and one small mark carries it everywhere (a lightning bolt on the header button, the closing call to action, the marquee).
- **Why:** an accent with a meaning becomes a signal; readers learn it in seconds.
- **Fails when:** the accent is used for decoration, or more than one accent competes.

### 15. Run one metaphor through name, words, icon and action
The offer's metaphor (a sprint) runs through everything: the product name ("Brand Sprints"), the headlines ("On your marks. Get set. Go-to-market."), the icon (a bolt), and the final button ("Sprint past the competition").
- **Why:** a single metaphor carried consistently is memorable and makes the offer feel designed, not described.
- **Fails when:** the metaphor is forced into every sentence, or puns replace plain facts.

### 16. State the offer, the audience and the outcome in the first line
"Blazing fast brand sprints for startups": what, how fast, for whom, in six words, over a full-bleed image of real work.
- **Fails when:** the first line is a mood ("We make brands sing") rather than an offer.

### 17. Draw the argument instead of saying it
A comparison becomes a chart: a short bar labelled *Them* and a long accent bar labelled *You*; benefits sit underneath as plain lines.
- **Why:** a visual comparison is understood before it's read.
- **Fails when:** the chart implies data that doesn't exist (no axis, no source), so keep it clearly illustrative or back it with real figures.

### 18. Sell outcomes, not services
Captions under the work and the marquee speak in outcomes ("Look 10x bigger →", "Get to market faster →", "Raise a round →"), not deliverables ("logo, guidelines, website").
- **Why:** buyers buy results; the work becomes proof of each outcome.

### 19. Turn lineage into a line
"Your journey is short because ours was long." Authority from experience, with a turn of phrase, then shown as a timeline from 2004 to now with the logos of past clients.
- **Why:** heritage reassures; the turn makes it about the client, not the studio.

### 20. Shopfront scarcity, only if true
Live availability in the header ("1 slot left for December") makes a studio feel like a place with a calendar.
- **Why:** real scarcity is useful information and a nudge.
- **Fails when:** it's fake or permanent. Then it's a dark pattern (see the ethics line in `rationale-template.md`).

### 21. A minimal hover in the accent
The work tile's small label tab grows into a full-width accent bar along the tile's base on hover. One move, in the one accent, on every tile.
- **Why:** quiet frames need only a small, consistent signal of interactivity.

### 22. End on one big action
The page closes on a single oversized call to action that repeats the metaphor and the mark (a pill with the bolt: "Sprint past the competition"), on a black field.
- **Why:** the end of the page is the last impression (peak-end); one clear action converts the momentum.

## How to use these

- Read alongside `SIGN-SYSTEM.md` and `BRAND-SYSTEM.md`: these lessons sharpen execution; they never override the personality. A precise financial brand applies lesson 1 with cool, quiet hues and lesson 3 with a restrained display face, not a chunky retro one.
- In the Stage E audit, ask of each screen: one temperature? one face shouting? at most three devices? proof qualified? does every pairing pass contrast? Is the accent's meaning clear, and does one metaphor run through name, words, icon and action?
